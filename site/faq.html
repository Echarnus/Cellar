Getting started

What Cellar is, and what it needs from your Mac.

What is Cellar?

Cellar runs Windows games on Apple Silicon Macs. For each game it builds a bottle — a small, self-contained Windows environment — from Wine plus a graphics-translation layer that turns the game's Direct3D calls into Metal. The game runs much as it does on a PC. Your Mac does not become one.

It is the same idea as Valve's Proton on Linux, aimed at macOS, and it is free and open source under GPL-3.0.

Do I need a Windows licence, Boot Camp, or a virtual machine?

None of the three. Wine is not an emulator and not a virtual machine — it answers the Windows API calls a game makes, as it makes them. There is no Windows install to maintain and no licence to buy.

Which Macs does it work on?

Apple Silicon only (M1 and later), on macOS 27 or newer — the newest macOS every M1 Mac can run, so no Apple Silicon Mac is left out. Cellar's Wine runners are x86 builds that run under Rosetta 2, with the graphics work handed to Metal on the Apple GPU — so an Intel Mac is out of scope.

Open the app, or run cellar doctor, and it will tell you what your machine is missing rather than failing later for an unclear reason.

How do I install it?

Download the DMG, drag Cellar to Applications, and open it. On first launch it introduces itself, asks once for the folders where Windows games keep their saves, and offers to sign you in to your stores. You can skip signing in — Cellar still opens, and signing in later works identically from Accounts (⌘⇧A) or Settings → Manage accounts.

Cellar downloads the Wine runner itself the first time a game needs one. No Terminal required, though there is a full cellar command-line tool if you prefer one.

macOS says Cellar can't be opened.

Cellar is free software and isn't signed with a paid Apple Developer ID, so Apple hasn't notarised it and macOS can't vouch for who made it. The first time you open it, macOS stops it once. On macOS 27 there is no Open button in that first message — right-clicking and choosing Open no longer skips it — so allow it from Settings:

  1. Drag Cellar to Applications and double-click it. macOS says “Cellar” Not Opened. Click Done — not Move to Trash.
  2. Open System Settings → Privacy & Security and scroll down to Security. You'll see “Cellar” was blocked to protect your Mac. Click Open Anyway. The button only shows for about an hour after step 1; if it's gone, do step 1 again.
  3. macOS asks once more. Click Open Anyway and confirm with your password or Touch ID.

You only do this once; after that Cellar opens normally, and its updates are checked against Cellar's own signing key. Before you do it, you can check the download is the one we published.

Don't use a Terminal command that removes the quarantine flag: it skips the same check for everything else it touches.

How do I know the download really is Cellar?

Every release publishes the SHA-256 checksum of each download, in SHA256SUMS.txt on the release page and under the Download button on this site. Open Terminal and run:

shasum -a 256 ~/Downloads/Cellar.dmg

The long line of letters and digits it prints must match the published one exactly. If it doesn't, don't open the file: delete it, download it again from this site or GitHub Releases, and tell us if it still doesn't match.

Get Cellar only from this site or the GitHub releases page. Any other copy is not ours.

Is it free? Do I need an account with you?

Free, GPL-3.0, and there is no Cellar account, no sign-up and no telemetry. The only accounts involved are the game stores you already have.

Accounts and signing in

Why Cellar asks, what it can see, and what it deliberately won't claim.

Why do I have to sign in at all?

Because the games are yours. Cellar does not host or distribute game files — it installs from your own Steam, Battle.net or GOG account, the way those clients would on a PC. No sign-in, no library.

Once per store, or once per game?

Once per store. One Steam sign-in covers your whole Steam library — both what you own and downloading it. GOG is one token Cellar holds for the account. That is why signing in lives on the Accounts screen — the person icon in the sidebar, ⌘⇧A, or Settings → Manage accounts — and not on each game's page.

Does Cellar see my password?

No. Steam's sign-in is Steam's own QR device flow: you approve it in the Steam mobile app. GOG uses its normal login page and hands back an OAuth token, which Cellar keeps in your macOS Keychain. Battle.net's sign-in happens entirely inside Blizzard's client.

Why does Battle.net say “Add” instead of “Sign in”?

Because Blizzard writes nothing readable that says who is signed in, or which games that account owns. Steam and GOG both answer those questions; Battle.net does not.

So Accounts asks you once whether Battle.net is yours — Add — and Cellar then shows its games, saying plainly that it cannot check you own them. You sign in inside Blizzard's own window when you open it. Where Cellar looks oddly modest about what it knows, this is usually why.

Why does a Windows Steam run inside the bottle?

Cellar downloads Steam games with your one sign-in, without starting a Steam window. But many Steamworks games check for a running Steam while they play, so for those Cellar installs the Windows Steam client in the bottle and starts it beside the game. It is a dependency of the game, not a second account. Cards that say Just the game under While playing skip it: the game still comes from your Steam account, it just doesn't need Steam running to play.

Can I sign out?

Yes. Accounts offers Sign out for each session Cellar itself holds. The Steam client and Battle.net are signed out from inside their own windows, because that is where those sessions actually live.

Games and compatibility

What the ratings mean, and why a game might be missing.

What do Playable, Experimental and Untested mean?
  • Playable — someone ran it on named hardware and the profile says which Mac.
  • Experimental — it runs, with caveats worth reading before you count on it.
  • Untested — the profile exists; nobody has confirmed it. Cellar says so rather than guessing.

Every card on the games list is generated from a profile in the repository, so this site cannot claim more than the profile does.

My game isn't listed. Can I still play it?

Often, yes — the list is what has been profiled, not the limit of what works. A profile is a small text file naming the game's store, its AppID or install folder, its executable and graphics API, and which backend to use. Copy the closest existing one and adjust it; see CONTRIBUTING.md.

Adding a profile is also how a game reaches this site: it is rebuilt from the profiles every time one changes.

What about games with anti-cheat?

Competitive games with kernel-level anti-cheat generally do not run, and Cellar will not make them. It never circumvents DRM or anti-cheat — protection runs through the translation layer untouched, exactly as the publisher shipped it. Where a profile knows of an anti-cheat problem it says so plainly, rather than letting you discover it after a 60 GB download.

Does the store's client have to be running while I play?

It depends on the game, and its card says which. Steamworks titles usually want a live Steam session. GOG's games are DRM-free, so nothing needs to run beside them. Either way the game is installed from your own store account; cards that say Just the game under While playing simply don't need the store's app running beside it.

Performance

What to expect, and the levers that exist.

How fast is it, really?

Expect a cost against the same hardware running a native game: you are paying for API translation, and for x86 code under Rosetta 2. How much depends far more on the game than on Cellar — CPU-bound simulation games feel it most, GPU-bound ones least.

Each profile's notes name the Mac the numbers came from, because “it runs well” means nothing without that.

What are D3DMetal and DXVK?

Two routes from Direct3D to Metal. D3DMetal is Apple's own translation layer (from the Game Porting Toolkit) and is usually the faster one. DXVK translates Direct3D to Vulkan, which MoltenVK then puts onto Metal — slower in most cases, but it works in places D3DMetal does not. Each profile names a backend and a fallback, so a game that misbehaves on one can be run on the other.

Can I see an FPS counter?

Yes — Settings → Show Metal performance overlay (FPS) adds an FPS and frametime HUD to games launched from Cellar. To measure a whole session and see what limits it, run cellar perf measure <game>.

Controllers and racing wheels

What reaches the game today, and the set-up that happens outside Cellar.

Can I play with a controller?

Usually, yes. Xbox, PlayStation (DualSense, DualShock 4) and Switch Pro controllers connect to your Mac the normal way — pair them in System Settings → Bluetooth, or plug them in over USB. There is nothing to install.

Wine then hands the pad to the game, as an Xbox controller where it can. Whether a given game plays well with one is up to the game: its page shows what the pad is called and what its profile records about controllers, and says so when nobody has recorded it yet. cellar controllers lists what your Mac can see right now.

macOS asks whether Cellar may “receive keystrokes from any application”.

Allow it. That is how macOS names Input Monitoring, and Wine needs it to read a controller or wheel at all: without it a pad your Mac sees never reaches the game.

If you said no, open System Settings → Privacy & Security → Input Monitoring, turn Cellar on (or your terminal, if you start games with the cellar command), then quit and reopen the game. cellar doctor shows where the setting stands.

My controller's buttons are wrong, or the game doesn't see it.
  • A Steam game started through Steam gets the pad through Steam Input, which can remap it. Change that in Steam's controller settings for the game: on, to make a PlayStation or Switch pad act as an Xbox one; off, to give the game the pad as it is.
  • Check Input Monitoring, as above.
  • Run cellar doctor. Its Controller layer line says whether the Wine runner can present PlayStation and Switch pads as Xbox ones; without that, an Xbox pad is the safest choice.
Can I use a racing wheel, like a Fanatec, Logitech or Thrustmaster?

Cellar recognises wheel bases, pedals and shifters from Fanatec, Logitech, Thrustmaster and MOZA, lists them on the game's page and in cellar controllers, and Wine passes them to the game as a Windows game controller. Steering, pedals and buttons can reach a racing game that way.

Force feedback depends on the wheel. macOS only drives a wheel's motor through a driver from the wheel's maker, and none of them ships one for Apple Silicon Macs. So Cellar brings its own: a build of SDL that talks to the wheel over USB, which Wine uses in place of the runner's.

  • Fanatec CSL DD, GT DD Pro, ClubSport DD, Podium DD1 and DD2, CSL Elite, ClubSport V2 and V2.5, CSR Elite and Porsche 911 — force feedback is driven by Cellar's own Fanatec driver. The base must be in PC mode.
  • Logitech G29, G27, G25, Driving Force GT and Driving Force Pro — force feedback is driven by Cellar.
  • Thrustmaster T300 RS, T300 Ferrari Edition, TX, T248, TS-XW and TS-PC — force feedback is driven by Cellar's own Thrustmaster driver. A T300 starts in a generic mode; Cellar switches it into its own mode when a game starts, the way Thrustmaster's Windows driver does, so it briefly disconnects and comes back.
  • MOZA, Thrustmaster's T150, T500 and TMX, and Logitech's G920 and G923 — steering, pedals and buttons, but no force feedback yet.

Cellar's drivers are new. If force feedback feels wrong, report it with the wheel and the game.

Fanatec bases start at half strength. A direct-drive base at full torque can hurt a hand, so Cellar caps it until you choose otherwise. The base's own force-feedback setting (FF in its tuning menu) still applies on top. To change it, open Cellar's Settings › General › Racing wheels and pick a strength; it applies to every wheel Cellar drives from a game's next launch (or cellar controllers strength 75). Logitech and Thrustmaster wheels start at the game's own strength. To turn Cellar's Fanatec and Thrustmaster drivers off, start the game with CELLAR_FFB_DISABLE=1.

The game's page and cellar controllers say which of these your wheel is. Results per wheel and per game are still being collected, and what has been tested is recorded in the game's profile.

What do I have to set up on the wheel itself?

Most of it happens outside Cellar, once, and some of it needs a Windows PC because the makers only publish their tools for Windows:

  • Fanatec — put the base in PC mode, not Compatibility (PlayStation/Xbox) mode; its manual shows how. Update the firmware first on a Windows PC with Fanatec's own software: Fanatec offers no Mac app, and a base on old firmware may not show up.
  • Logitech — macOS recognises the G29 and G920 by itself, with nothing to install. Firmware updates go through Logitech G HUB; if it doesn't list your wheel on the Mac, update it once on a Windows PC.
  • Thrustmaster — set the base to its PC mode and connect it over USB. Thrustmaster's drivers and firmware are Windows-only, and without the driver some bases report the pedals as buttons, which most racing games cannot use. There is nothing to install for force feedback: Cellar switches a T300 into the right mode itself.
  • MOZA — update the firmware and set the steering angle in MOZA Pit House on a Windows PC first; the base keeps those settings.

Then plug the wheel into the Mac, allow Input Monitoring when macOS asks, and pick the wheel in the game's own controls menu.

When something goes wrong

The usual causes, in the order worth checking.

The first set-up is taking ages. Is it stuck?

Probably not. The first game downloads a Wine runner and builds a fresh bottle, which takes a few minutes on a good connection; later games reuse the runner and are much quicker. Cellar shows what it is running while it works — if the activity log is still moving, let it finish.

A store window opened and is waiting for me.

Expected, for Battle.net: Blizzard's installer has no silent mode, so its window genuinely needs a few clicks. Cellar warns you before opening one rather than appearing to hang and being force-quit. Steam's installer does run silently.

The game won't start.
  • Read the game's page — it names the step it is actually waiting on.
  • Check you are signed in to that game's store (Accounts, ⌘⇧A).
  • Try the profile's fallback backend if the failure looks graphical.
  • Run cellar doctor for a machine-level check.
Where are the logs?

Settings → Open logs folder, or on disk at ~/Library/Application Support/Cellar/logs. Everything Cellar keeps — runners, bottles, caches and logs — lives under that one folder, which Settings can also open for you.

How do I report a problem?

Open an issue on GitHub. The details that help: your Mac and macOS version, the game and its profile, which backend you used, and the log from the run that failed.

How do I remove a game, or Cellar itself?

cellar uninstall <game>, or the ••• menu on the game's page in the app. Cellar shows the plan first — every path it will delete, with its size, and what it will keep — and asks before anything goes. Add --dry-run to see that plan without deleting anything.

What it keeps: your store sign-in, the Wine runner, and the shared Steam install with every other game in it, so re-installing is just the download. Add --bottle to take the bottle too — its Wine prefix, registry and the store client inside it.

To remove Cellar entirely, cellar reset --everything. Cellar does not live in one folder — besides ~/Library/Application Support/Cellar it writes launcher apps into ~/Applications, adds entries to your Steam library, and keeps a GOG sign-in in your login keychain. That command takes all of it, plus the app and the cellar command themselves, and shows you the list first. Without --everything it stops short of the app and the command, so you can carry on using Cellar.

Legal and licensing

What Cellar ships, and what it deliberately does not.

Is this legal?

Cellar is a compatibility layer. It runs software you own, from accounts you already have, and it does not modify, crack or bypass any protection. Wine has been doing exactly this, lawfully, for decades.

Does Cellar bundle Apple's D3DMetal?

No, and deliberately. D3DMetal comes under Apple's non-commercial terms, so Cellar never redistributes it: it is grafted into a runner on your own machine, at run time, from components already there. The same rule covers the store icons in the app: they are borrowed from the copies already on your Mac, or drawn in code — never shipped as logo files.

Is Cellar affiliated with Valve, Blizzard, GOG or Apple?

No. Cellar is an independent project. Store and product names appear only to say where a game came from.

What licence is it under?

GPL-3.0. The source, the profile database and this site all live in the repository.

Still stuck?

Open an issue on GitHub with your Mac, your macOS version, the game, and the log from the run that failed — that is usually enough to spot it. Or browse the 10 profiled games.
